1Introduction & Executive Summary

Six months ago, our 2025 year-end report made four central calls: residential construction would stay muted until interest rates eased, tariffs were the cost threat to watch, federal infrastructure programs would keep nonresidential demand resilient, and merger activity would stay hot. Three of the four held — and the exceptions are as instructive as the hits.

H1 2026 was a half of divergence. Rate-sensitive residential stayed weak and choppy; nonresidential held up — but the engine was a private, AI-driven data-center construction boom, not the federal programs we emphasized. Material costs escalated hard on Section 232 tariffs, then partially reversed in June, leaving cost planning hostage to policy. And the corporate story of the half was consolidation, capped by Berkshire Hathaway's bet on American housing.

Key risks for H2 2026

Rate-gated recovery — now may reverse

Mortgage rates stayed in the low-6% range but volatile; the June 17 Fed hold turned hawkish under new chair Warsh, and relief now looks like a 2027 story.

Tariff & policy whipsaw

The cost line now moves with policy in both directions. The uncertainty itself is the planning risk.

Demand concentration

Data centers are a real tailwind — but concentrate exposure in a single capital-, power- and politically-constrained source.

Affordability ceiling

A ~$440K median new-home price and fragile confidence cap any organic residential rebound.

3Market Context & Outlook

The H1 2026 backdrop. The macroeconomy softened rather than firmed. Q4 2025 GDP was revised down sharply to 0.7% from a 1.4% advance, and the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow tracked Q1 2026 down to roughly 1.6% from an initial 3.1%. Recession-probability estimates spanned 20–42%. Consumer confidence cratered to a 12-year low of 84.5 in January before clawing back to 91.8 by March.

Outlook for H2 2026. We carry three lessons forward: the residential recovery is delayed and rate-gated — after the hawkish June 17 hold the base case is now 2027, with a real chance rates rise before they fall (we won't anchor its timing to Fed-cut expectations again); tariffs remain the swing factor on cost, but the frame is now policy volatility; and data centers are the most reliable demand thread into the back half, even as consolidation reshapes procurement leverage for every manufacturer.

① Interest & Mortgage Rates — the call we missed

The Fed held at 3.50–3.75% twice (March 18, then a unanimous June 17 hold — the fourth straight), and the 30-year fixed mortgage stayed in the low-6% range but unusually volatile — climbing five straight weeks to 6.46%, falling to 6.20% within days, then edging to 6.36%. The June meeting turned hawkish under new chair Kevin Warsh: forward guidance dropped, the end-2026 inflation projection raised to 3.6%, and the dot-plot median lifted to ~3.6–4.1% with nine of 18 projecting a hike. Our easing-into-recovery base case did not play out; new-home sales slumped in April. Implication: the rate story flipped from delayed easing to a credible hike risk — plan off a 2027 recovery, not a back-half-2026 one.

③ Material & Energy Costs — the lead lever, under-called

Our strongest call, understated. Section 232 tariffs reached 50% on steel, aluminum and copper (eff. April 2–6). Producer prices rose 4.0% YoY in March; nonres construction inputs surged at a 12.6% annualized rate; hot-rolled coil steel breached $1,000/ton (+23% off January lows). Brookings put the hit at ~$17,500 per new home. Then came a June partial reversal. Implication: costs are the headline lever, and the risk is the escalation-then-reversal whipsaw.

④ Demand Visibility — split the story

Residential was choppy and ultimately soft (March starts +10.8%, April −2.8% to 1.47M SAAR, May starts and permits both down more than expected). Nonresidential held — but the driver was data centers, not federal programs. The ABI stabilized below the growth line (48.5 → 43.8 → 49.4). Implication: report residential as rate-gated chop and break data-center-led nonres out as its own thread.

The four refresh levers: Labor — still tight, first cracks (ISM Services employment contracting). Infrastructure — $430B+ federal floor intact, eclipsed by private data-center capex. Credit — restrictive for small/spec, large caps raised freely (CEMEX $3B revolver). GDP/Sentiment — the gap persisted, now closing downward.

5Data-Center & AI-Infrastructure Demand

The defining demand surprise of the half — and the clearest growth story in the sector. AI data-center construction pulled demand through nearly every tracked segment.

Power, water, and rising local opposition — Ohio suspended a data-center tax break; activism in IL, TX, AZ.

For our clientsExposure to data-center demand — direct or pull-through into HVAC, electrical, structural and finishing products — is the cleanest growth opportunity in the sector, but it concentrates risk in a single, capital- and power-intensive demand source.

6The Consolidation Wave (M&A)

⭐ The headline: Berkshire's housing betOn May 31, Berkshire Hathaway agreed to acquire homebuilder Taylor Morrison for ~$8.5B — all-cash at $72.50/share, a ~24% premium, for a builder with 350+ communities across 21 markets. The significance is the buyer: a famously conservative, long-horizon institution making a direct, premium bet on U.S. homebuilding at a cyclical low. CNBC called it a signal the market "may have bottomed." Paired with Berkshire's Nucor stake, the message is that patient capital is going big on American construction.

Right on cost, labor, credit, the GDP gap, and M&A; early on housing; missed the data-center engine.
